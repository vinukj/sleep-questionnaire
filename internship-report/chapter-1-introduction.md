# INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 BACKGROUND

Sleep has emerged as a critical performance metric in athletic performance, occupational safety, and overall human health optimization. The relationship between sleep quality and cognitive-physical performance has been extensively documented across diverse populations, from elite athletes to shift workers, making sleep assessment an essential component of modern healthcare and performance optimization protocols.

### 1.1.1 Sleep as a Performance Metric in Athletics

The recognition of sleep as a fundamental pillar of athletic performance has transformed how sports medicine professionals approach training regimens and recovery protocols. Research conducted across multiple sporting disciplines has demonstrated that sleep extension and optimization can significantly improve athletic outcomes including reaction time, accuracy, sprint performance, and overall competitive success.

Elite athletes require heightened attention to sleep due to the demanding nature of their training schedules, frequent travel across time zones, and the physiological stress of competition. Studies have shown that sleep deprivation in athletes leads to decreased glycogen synthesis, impaired muscle recovery, reduced motivation, and increased injury risk. The correlation between sleep duration and injury incidence has been particularly striking, with adolescent athletes sleeping less than 8 hours per night demonstrating 1.7 times higher odds of experiencing sports-related injuries compared to their well-rested counterparts.

The paradigm shift toward recognizing sleep as a trainable, optimizable performance variable has led to the integration of sleep monitoring into standard athletic training programs. However, the tools currently employed for sleep assessment in athletic populations remain largely adapted from general clinical instruments, lacking sport-specific and culturally relevant validation.

### 1.1.2 Sleep Disorder Prevalence in Indian Population

Sleep disorders represent a significant yet underrecognized public health burden in India. Epidemiological studies indicate that obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), insomnia, and circadian rhythm disorders affect substantial portions of the Indian population, with prevalence rates varying significantly across demographic groups.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) affects approximately 13.7% to 19.5% of the Indian adult population, with higher prevalence observed in urban populations and individuals with comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes. The phenotypic expression of OSA in Indian populations differs from Western cohorts, with craniofacial anatomical factors playing a more prominent role compared to obesity-related mechanisms. This distinction has important implications for screening instrument design and risk stratification algorithms.

Insomnia symptoms are reported by 18.5% to 24.3% of Indian adults, with higher rates among shift workers, healthcare professionals, and students. The cultural context of sleep in India—including multigenerational sleeping arrangements, variable sleep-wake schedules influenced by religious practices, and climate-related sleep disruptions—necessitates assessment tools that capture culturally specific sleep experiences.

Despite the substantial disease burden, sleep medicine remains an underdeveloped specialty in India, with fewer than 500 certified sleep specialists serving a population of over 1.4 billion. The majority of sleep laboratories are concentrated in metropolitan centers, leaving rural and semi-urban populations without access to diagnostic services. This disparity underscores the need for scalable pre-screening tools that can identify high-risk individuals for prioritized referral.

### 1.1.3 Limitations of Standardized Sleep Assessment Tools (PSQI, ESS)

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) represent the most widely used instruments for sleep assessment in both clinical and research settings globally. These tools have demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in Western populations and have been translated into multiple languages including Hindi and other Indian languages.

However, significant limitations emerge when these instruments are applied to Indian populations without appropriate cultural adaptation:

Linguistic Equivalence Issues: Direct translation fails to capture conceptual equivalence. Terms such as "restless sleep," "sleep latency," and "daytime sleepiness" carry different connotations across languages and cultural contexts. The concept of "feeling rested upon awakening" may be interpreted differently in cultures where gradual awakening through multiple alarms is normative.

Cultural Sleep Practices: Standard instruments assume Western sleep patterns—single consolidated nocturnal sleep episodes in private bedrooms. Indian sleep practices often include daytime rest periods, multigenerational bedroom sharing, and variable sleep schedules influenced by occupational, religious, and familial obligations. These practices are pathologized by standard instruments rather than understood as culturally normative variations.

Symptom Expression Differences: The manifestation and reporting of sleep symptoms vary across cultures. Indian patients may emphasize somatic complaints (headaches, body aches) over cognitive symptoms (concentration difficulties, memory problems) when describing sleep-related impairment. Standard instruments weighted toward cognitive-affective symptoms may underestimate sleep disorder severity in Indian populations.

Athlete-Specific Gaps: Neither PSQI nor ESS addresses sleep factors particularly relevant to athletes, including pre-competition anxiety, travel-related sleep disruption, training load effects, and the impact of performance supplements on sleep architecture. The Athletic Sleep Strategies Questionnaire (ASSQ) and Athlete Sleep Behavior Questionnaire (ASBQ) have been developed for Western athletic populations but lack validation in Indian contexts.

Scoring Threshold Disparities: Cut-off scores established in Western populations may not optimize sensitivity and specificity in Indian cohorts. Research on the Indian version of the ESS (ESS-I) suggests that lower threshold scores may be appropriate for detecting excessive daytime sleepiness in Indian populations.

These limitations collectively argue for the development and validation of culturally adapted, population-specific sleep assessment instruments that maintain psychometric rigor while capturing culturally relevant sleep experiences and symptom presentations.

## 1.2 MOTIVATION

The development of this integrated sleep assessment platform emerged from direct observation of clinical workflow challenges at St. John's Medical College Hospital, Bangalore, combined with recognition of the technological gaps in current sleep assessment practices in India.

### 1.2.1 Clinical Workflow Challenges at St. John's Hospital

During the clinical rotation at St. John's Sleep Disorders Laboratory, several critical inefficiencies in the patient assessment workflow were identified:

Manual Data Handling: Patient questionnaire responses were collected on paper forms, requiring manual transcription into electronic health records. This process introduced transcription errors, delayed risk assessment, and created barriers to data aggregation for research purposes. A typical patient intake involved 6-8 different paper forms, each requiring separate handling and filing.

Fragmented Assessment Process: Patients completed multiple questionnaires (PSQI, ESS, STOP-BANG, Berlin Questionnaire) in sequence, with no integration of results. Clinicians manually synthesized findings from disparate sources, a process prone to cognitive bias and information loss. The absence of automated scoring meant that risk stratification occurred only after the clinician reviewed the complete file, often days after initial presentation.

Lack of Decision Support: Junior residents and fellows lacked access to evidence-based decision support during patient evaluations. Treatment recommendations were based on individual clinician experience rather than standardized guidelines, leading to practice variation and potential under-treatment of high-risk patients.

Document Burden: Voice dictation for clinical notes required transcription services with 48-72 hour turnaround times, delaying documentation completion and billing. The cognitive load of simultaneous patient interaction and note-taking reduced the quality of patient-clinician communication.

Data Silos: Polysomnography (PSG) data stored in proprietary EDF (European Data Format) files remained disconnected from questionnaire responses, preventing integrated analysis of subjective-objective sleep correlations. The inability to automatically correlate patient-reported sleep quality with objective PSG parameters limited both clinical insight and research capability.

These workflow challenges resulted in delayed diagnosis, increased administrative burden, and suboptimal utilization of limited sleep laboratory resources. The motivation to address these challenges through technology-driven solutions formed the foundation of this project.

### 1.2.2 Need for Culturally Validated Assessment Instruments

The second driving force behind this project stems from the recognition that existing sleep assessment tools, while psychometrically validated in Western contexts, fail to capture the sleep experiences of Indian populations accurately.

Clinical Observation: During patient interviews at St. John's, it became evident that standard questionnaires missed culturally specific sleep concerns. Patients frequently described sleep experiences using culturally rooted terminology—"heavy sleep," "dream fatigue," "morning grogginess from late-night family obligations"—that had no correspondence in standard instruments.

Research Gap: A systematic review of Indian sleep literature revealed that no validated athlete-specific sleep questionnaire existed for Indian populations. Given India's growing investment in elite sports programs and the recognition of sleep as a performance factor, this gap represented a significant barrier to evidence-based sports medicine practice.

Healthcare Equity: The concentration of sleep specialists in urban centers means that rural patients often present with advanced disease, having been undiagnosed for years. A digital pre-screening tool that can be administered in primary care settings or via mobile devices could dramatically improve early detection and appropriate referral patterns.

Technology Opportunity: The widespread adoption of smartphones and internet connectivity in India creates an unprecedented opportunity for digital health interventions. A culturally adapted, digitally administered assessment platform could reach populations previously excluded from specialized sleep care.

### 1.2.3 Digital Pre-screening for Resource Optimization

The third motivation derives from health economics considerations. Sleep laboratories in India operate under severe resource constraints, with long waiting lists for diagnostic polysomnography. A digital pre-screening system that can accurately stratify patient risk enables:

Prioritized Scheduling: High-risk OSA patients can be fast-tracked for in-laboratory PSG, while low-risk patients with alternative sleep complaints can be directed to appropriate specialists without consuming limited PSG slots.

Reduced No-Show Rates: Digital engagement prior to the clinical visit improves patient investment in the assessment process, reducing appointment no-shows that represent significant revenue loss for sleep laboratories.

Enhanced Data Quality: Automated scoring and real-time validation eliminate transcription errors and ensure complete data capture, improving the reliability of clinical decisions based on questionnaire results.

Research Infrastructure: Digitally captured data creates a foundation for longitudinal research, quality improvement initiatives, and population health analytics that are impossible with paper-based systems.

## 1.3 SCOPE OF THE PROJECT

This project focuses on the design and development of an integrated digital platform for sleep disorder assessment tailored to the clinical workflow at St. John's Sleep Disorders Laboratory. The scope encompasses both frontend and backend systems that address patient intake, risk assessment, clinical documentation, and decision support.

### 1.3.1 Inclusions

The following components are within the project scope:

Authentication System: Keycloak-based OIDC authentication with single-device session enforcement, Google OAuth integration, and hybrid support for legacy bcrypt-hashed credentials.

Dynamic Questionnaire Engine: Schema-driven assessment forms with conditional question rendering, automatic BMI and anthropometric calculations, and localStorage-based form persistence.

ML Risk Prediction: Integration with a Python-based OSA prediction service that processes patient responses and returns risk scores with graceful degradation on service failure.

RAG-Based Decision Support: Evidence retrieval from clinical guidelines using ChromaDB vector store with local embeddings, generating cited explanations for risk assessments.

Voice Transcription: Real-time audio capture using Azure Speech Services with speaker diarization, multi-language support (9 Indian languages), and structured JSON output via Gemini AI.

Data Export: Excel export with formula injection prevention and OCR processing for digitizing paper records using Tesseract.js and mammoth.

Admin Dashboard: Patient record management with search, filtering, pagination, and response viewing capabilities.

### 1.3.2 Exclusions

The following are explicitly outside the project scope:

Direct PSG/EDF Integration: Reading, parsing, or visualizing polysomnography data is not included in the current implementation.

Wearable Device Integration: APIs for Fitbit, Oura, Garmin, or other consumer sleep trackers are not implemented.

Mobile Application: No native mobile apps (iOS/Android) are developed; the platform is web-based only.

Direct EHR Integration: The system does not integrate with hospital EHR systems for automated patient data exchange.

Diagnostic Classification: The platform provides risk scores and decision support but does not make diagnostic determinations.

Telemedicine Features: Video consultation, remote patient monitoring, or patient portals are not included.

### 1.3.3 Limitations

Single-Center Deployment: The system is deployed and validated only at St. John's Sleep Disorders Laboratory, Bangalore.

OSA-Focused Prediction: The ML model predicts only Obstructive Sleep Apnea risk, not other sleep disorders (narcolepsy, restless leg syndrome, circadian disorders).

English-Only Interface: The user interface is in English, though voice transcription supports multiple Indian languages.

Internet Dependency: Voice transcription requires continuous internet connectivity for Azure Speech Services.

Single-Server Architecture: All services run on a single VPS, creating a single point of failure without redundancy.
